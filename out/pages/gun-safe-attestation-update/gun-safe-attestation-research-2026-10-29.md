## 3. T1 — Safes under 1,500 lb: the same careful way

### Domain depth

Gun-safe weight is driven by size, steel gauge (lower number = thicker steel), and fireboard layers. Published domain guides put entry-level safes at 200–600 lb, mid-range at 600–1,000 lb, high-end at 1,000–1,600 lb, and commercial-grade at 2,000–5,000 lb; a size chart puts small rifle safes at 80–300 lb, medium at 300–900 lb, and large at 550–1,100 lb, and a 24-gun safe anywhere from about 300 lb to well over 1,500 lb depending on construction. The lesson for search is that capacity labels do not fix weight: two safes with the same gun count can differ by hundreds of pounds, which is why the quote asks for make, model, or approximate weight, or size and location when weight is unknown.

Why care does not scale down with weight: a safe is tall, narrow, and top-heavy, with no good hand-holds, and its whole load concentrates on a few square inches of floor. A 500 lb safe still needs a trained team and rated equipment, and its floor concentration still matters more than its total weight. The hub already says this in its own words ("Standard heavy care" for under 1,500 lb; "a safe concentrates its whole load on a few square inches of floor — which is why the floor check matters more than the lifting"), and the attested answer confirms it as company practice rather than as a hub implication.

The domain pattern is: move one step at a time, with control from above and below (going down, the person above controls descent while the person below brakes and guides; going up, the push comes from below while the person above pulls and guides), never rush, stop immediately if the load shifts. ## 7. T5 — Re-anchoring: if the client wants it

### Domain depth

Anchoring is what makes a safe a safe rather than a heavy box: a freestanding safe can be carried away when it cannot be opened on the spot, which is why guides state plainly that safes are not truly secure against theft unless bolted down. Most safes ship with pre-drilled anchor holes in the floor of the safe, covered by floor caps and a floor panel. The domain procedure, from manufacturer guides, is: position the empty safe, mark the floor through the holes, move the safe aside, drill, clean out dust, reposition, insert anchors, tighten incrementally and evenly without over-tightening, and test by rocking the safe — it should be immobile.

Anchor type follows the floor. Concrete takes a hammer drill and concrete anchors (wedge or sleeve anchors; one manufacturer guide specifies a 3/8-inch concrete bit for its concrete kit). A wood subfloor takes lag bolts with washers, driven to reach the joists (one guide specifies a 1/4-inch pilot hole and a 9/16-inch socket for its wood kit). Tile is the caution case: a safe can sit on tile if the weight is evenly distributed, but bolting into tile can crack it, so tile anchoring is a case for care, not a routine step. Post-tension slabs are the danger case: steel cables under thousands of pounds of pressure run through the slab, and damaging one can snap it out of the concrete; a slab stamp identifies a post-tension floor, and doubt means asking the builder. None of that is company instruction — it is the domain background that explains why the quote asks for floor type, and why re-anchoring is on request rather than automatic: the floor decides the method, and the client decides whether the safe is bolted down at the destination at all.

### Domain depth: the class spectrum

Construction ratings describe build: B-rate typically means about 1/4-inch steel walls with a 1/2-inch door, C-rate steps up to about 1/2-inch walls with a 1-inch door, and steel gauge runs backwards (lower number = thicker steel; 12-gauge is about 0.1 inches, 18-gauge about 0.05 inches). Tested ratings describe performance under attack, measured as net working time: UL RSC (Residential Security Container) resisted common hand tools for a five-minute net working time and is the baseline third-party benchmark for residential safes, not a fortress rating; TL-15 resisted power tools for 15 minutes of net working time; TL-30 for 30 minutes; TL-30x6 extends the 30-minute test to all six sides; TRTL adds torch resistance. Insurance-facing guidelines (from safe retailers, labeled as guidelines only, not guarantees) associate higher ratings with higher insurable content values, but no such value claim is made for this company anywhere in this package.

For search, the important phrasing distinction is that most residential gun safes do NOT carry TL ratings, so a searcher asking about TL-15/TL-30 is usually asking about a genuinely different weight and security class from a standard residential gun safe — which is exactly why the quote asks for the rating rather than assuming it.

## 12. T10 — Locksmith: NO locksmith services

### Domain depth

Locksmith safe work, from locksmith guides, covers four situations searchers actually face: a forgotten combination (manufacturer recovery with proof of ownership and serial number, or a locksmith opening); a dead electronic keypad (fresh batteries first, then lock or keypad replacement); a combination change (mechanical dial recombination, digital code reset following the manufacturer's manual, recommended after a security concern, a move, or a change of ownership); and a failed or jammed lock (manipulation first, drilling at manufacturer points only when necessary, then repair of the drill point so the safe locks securely again).
