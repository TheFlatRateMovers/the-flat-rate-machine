## 1. What office moving IS (domain facts, researched + company facts, verified)

- **Office moving** = relocating a working office — desks and workstations, files and records runs, and office equipment — from one premises to another, in a planned order, so the team can sit down and work at the new address. The company's published definition (hub page, verified live 2026-10-29): "Complete office relocations, quoted flat and agreed in writing: desks and workstations, files and office equipment, moved in a planned order so your team can sit down and work at the new address."
- **Office types in scope (company-published + domain):** professional offices, shop offices, commercial spaces, and industrial offices inside plants, warehouses, and industrial facilities. Audience (hub page, verbatim scope): "Office managers, business owners, and facility teams moving a professional office, a shop office, or an industrial office inside a plant, warehouse, or industrial facility — where access, floor space, staging, and equipment handling must be planned around the site itself." Industrial offices have their own category and node — this package treats them as a cross-reference, not a duplication.
- **The planning unit is the operation, not the truck (company-published):** "We plan the order with you — what leaves first, what arrives first, and where each workstation, file run and piece of equipment lands." The commercial hub states the same rule for all business moves: "what moves first, what moves last, and what your team needs working on day one." That three-part rule (first / last / day-one) is the company's published planning spine, and the datasets below are built on it.
- **Staging is published (company fact):** "Full or partial packing for offices, staging at our warehouse when the dates don't line up neatly, and placement at the new site in the order your team needs it." The warehouse and transload operation is the published staging base: "freight and equipment handled between truck, container and warehouse, on the same job, under one roof." No staging duration, volume limit, or price is published — none is invented.
- **What office moving is NOT (boundaries, stated so the datasets do not overclaim):** it is not IT relocation as such (servers, racks, network equipment in a planned labelled sequence belong to the IT node — cross-referenced only); not industrial project support as such (warehouse teams and project support belong to the industrial-commercial node — cross-referenced only); not government or military moving as such (own categories — cross-referenced only); not packing-only (packing is its own service); not storage (unverified — see residential §8 Q1, which also gates any office staging beyond the published warehouse-staging sentence); not a downtime guarantee, SLA, or availability promise (none published — see §8).

## 2. Who needs it (buyer personas, researched + GSC evidence)

- **Office managers** — the person who owns the move day-to-day: floor plans, seating charts, labeling standards, packing deadlines by department, and the Day-1 checklist. Domain checklist (American Van Lines commercial relocation guide): final floor plans and seating charts with accessibility notes; IT cutover schedule, system freeze windows, and device packing rules; labeling standards and packing deadlines by department; building access, badging, elevator reservations, parking, and loading dock instructions.
- **Business owners / owner-operators** — the person who owns the cost question. Their fear is not the truck; it is the hidden-costs fear and the downtime bill. Domain math (labeled, never company figures): ITIC's 2024 survey found a single hour of downtime tops $300,000 for more than 90% of mid-size and large enterprises; Gartner reports $5,600 per minute in downtime during IT transitions; a $10M company faces roughly $55,000 per day; per-employee disruption runs $100–$400 per employee per day (industry estimates). The company's flat-rate-in-writing answer maps directly onto the hidden-costs fear: one agreed price before move day, no hourly meter running over a weekend move.
- **Facility / building managers** — the person who owns access: elevator reservations, loading dock windows, parking, badging, move hours, and certificates of insurance where managed buildings require them. Domain practice (Safebound commercial timeline): freight elevator windows typically 4–6 hours; multi-tenant buildings commonly restrict moves to evenings and weekends; loading dock reservations are mandatory in multi-tenant buildings. COI issuance itself is a company attestation question (§8 Q2) — domain practice only, never a company claim here.
- **IT coordinators** — the person who owns the systems question: servers, networks, and workstations must be backed up, labeled, safely transported, and tested immediately to avoid operational disruption (domain consensus across mover guides). IT scope here is a cross-reference to the IT node only: the IT page's published scope is servers, racks, workstations, and network equipment moved in a planned, labelled sequence so systems come back up in order. No IT disconnect/reconnect scope is claimed inside office datasets beyond that cross-reference.
- **Operations teams and department heads** — the people who own continuity: which departments move first, which stay working, and what must be working on day one. Domain phasing rule (American Van Lines): phase by function and risk — non-customer-facing teams midweek, then customer support and finance over a weekend with a defined blackout period; swing space and remote work as safety valves.

"virginia flat rate office moving company" at 1.0 and "business moving companies near me" at 1.0 are thin #1s to defend. ## 3. How it is done (the process chain, researched + matched to published company steps)

Domain process (researched 2026-10-29, from multiple commercial mover guides — stated as industry practice, not company claims), with the company's published steps matched at each stage:

1. **Survey and written quote** — a site walkthrough of both buildings documents furniture quantities, equipment types, access points, elevator availability, and loading dock access; the written quote follows from that scope (domain: Safebound delivers the written quote within 48 hours of walkthrough; Element requires the proposal to be written against a walkthrough of both buildings on volume, labor, travel, and materials/services). Company-published version: "Tell us the rooms or facility size in the Detailed Project form and we quote from the real scope" (hub page); quote toggle Short (Quick Quote) default / Detailed Project, inputs preserved; one flat price agreed in writing before move day.
2. **Plan the order** — the move plan states what leaves first, what arrives first, and where each workstation, file run, and piece of equipment lands (company-published spine). Domain detail: floor plans and seating charts finalized; labeling standards set by department (or by workstation/floor plan); packing deadlines set by department; IT cutover schedule and system freeze windows agreed (domain: American Van Lines).
3. **Pack and label** — full or partial packing (company-published); department/floor-plan labeling so every box and piece lands at the correct workstation (domain: color-coded labels by department, floor, or room number); employees pack personal desk contents or the crew handles everything (domain variant — the company publishes full/partial packing without an employee-packing rule, so no employee-packing claim is made here).
4. **Stage** — where the dates do not line up neatly, freight stages at the company's warehouse/transload operation (company-published); staging is the buffer between out and in, on the same job, under one roof. No staging duration or volume is published — none is stated.
5. **Sequenced move (phased where the size demands it)** — departments move in planned stages instead of everything at once (domain: phased moves let essential teams keep working while other areas are packed, moved, or set up); trucks load in reverse delivery order so the first items off are the first items needed (domain: Safebound). Company-published rule: "Do you move in a sequence, or all at once? In a sequence" (hub FAQ). Phasing models are D4; the company publishes sequence, not a specific phasing model — no model is claimed as company practice.
6. **Place and install** — furniture placed according to the floor plan, workstations/cubicles reassembled, equipment positioned for reconnection (domain). Company-published: "placement at the new site in the order your team needs it." IT reconnection itself belongs to the IT cross-reference, not to office datasets.
7. **Systems check and day-one working order** — the team sits down and works at the new address (company-published outcome). Domain detail: IT in "shadow mode" 24–48 hours (pre-stage network gear, test VLANs and Wi-Fi, pre-label ports and assets, floor-walking help desk on Day 1 — American Van Lines); KPIs tracked through the first week (tickets resolved same day, login success rates, call wait times, daily stabilization update — domain practice, never a company SLA here).
8. **Walkthrough and close-out** — a final walkthrough confirms every item is placed correctly before signoff (domain: Safebound); packing materials and debris removed (domain step; debris scope for office moves is an attestation question, §8 Q8, not a company claim).

Planning calendars that drive the chain (domain, from Gorilla Movers + Safe Ship + Cloudswitched): start 8–12 weeks out for most business relocations (12 weeks for 20+ workstations; 4–6 months for a full floor or multi-floor build); work backward from the first day of operations in the new space, not from move day; two external calendars do not flex — lease dates at both ends, and lead times for internet circuits, movers, and building elevators. Size-band lead times (Cloudswitched IT timeline, domain): small (<20 people) 4–6 weeks; medium (20–50) 6–10 weeks; large (50–100) 10–14 weeks; enterprise (100+) 14–20 weeks. None of these windows is a company promise — they are domain planning guidance, labeled as such.
