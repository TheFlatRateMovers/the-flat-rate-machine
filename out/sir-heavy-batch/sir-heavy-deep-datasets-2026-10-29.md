## 1. What SIR / KIM-GS IS (published company method + labeled domain context)

- **SIR — Structural Integrity Relocation.** The company's published definition (hub + protocol pages, verbatim substance): the method applied to high-mass assets of 1,500 to 3,500 lbs, executed under the KIM-GS Protocol and quoted at one flat rate in writing. The hub's framing: "1,500–3,500 lbs is not a 'big item.' It's an engineering job" — "a floor, a staircase, or a priceless asset should never be a guess." Every SIR move starts with the building, not the item.
- **KIM-GS — Kinetic Integrity Method (KIM-GS Protocol).** The protocol under which SIR moves are executed. Published content names the protocol and its steps (§3); it does not publish the protocol's internal calculations, models, or formulas. Nothing in this package fills that interior in — the published steps ARE the publishable method surface (§8 Q10 confirms whether that surface is complete).
- **The band: 1,500–3,500 lbs (published, everywhere).** The live file carries "1,500" 207 times and "3,500" 215 times. Assets below 1,500 lbs and above 3,500 lbs are NOT covered by a published SIR claim: sub-band handling (does the method apply below 1,500, as a separate handling, or not at all?) and above-band handling (accepted? limits?) are both gated (§8 Q1, Q2), never assumed from the band's existence.
- **L/360 floor deflection (published + domain meaning).** Published: "floor deflection analysis to L/360" and "floor deflection assessed against L/360 — span divided by 360 — before any lift is planned." Domain meaning (researched 2026-10-29, IRC Table R502.3.1): L/360 is the standard residential floor deflection limit — the allowable bend under live load equals the joist span (L) divided by 360. A 10-foot (120-inch) span may deflect about one-third of an inch; a 12-foot span 0.40 inches; a 15-foot span 0.50 inches. Tile and stone floors demand the stricter L/480. Stiffness, not ultimate strength, is usually the limiting factor for residential floors. The deflection formula is Δ = 5wL⁴/384EI. All of this paragraph's code content is domain education; the company's published claim is the L/360 check itself, not any of these figures.
- **Why a band like this needs a method at all (domain arithmetic, labeled illustrative).** IRC design loads for residential floors are 40 psf live load in living areas (30 psf sleeping areas, 20 psf uninhabitable attic storage) plus 10–20 psf dead load — roughly 50 psf total, as a UNIFORM load. A heavy asset does not load a floor uniformly: it concentrates its weight on a small footprint. Illustrative arithmetic (footprint assumed for illustration only, not a company figure): a 3,000-lb asset on a small footprint produces local pressures in the hundreds of psf, far above the 40 psf uniform design load. That is why the published method assesses the structure before the asset moves, spreads the load, and checks deflection — the uniform-load assumption that works for furniture and people does not work for a 3,000-lb safe, piano, or medical device. Concentrated loads require specific framing consideration under the code framework (e.g., doubled joists under heavy fixtures is standard practice for tubs) — domain context, not a company claim.
- **What SIR is NOT (boundaries, stated so the datasets do not overclaim):** it is not a certification, credential, or standards-body program (none published — §8 Q3); it is not a substitute for structural engineering (whether a licensed structural engineer is ever involved is gated — §8 Q5; domain guidance says unusual loads, spans near maximum, or high snow loads warrant a licensed structural engineer — labeled domain, not company practice); it is not a price, a crew-size rule, or an equipment list (none published beyond "dollies, straps and equipment" + warehouse forklift/track loader — §8 Q4, Q9); it is not medical-device or cryogenic expertise as such (those are separate categories; SIR datasets list them only as published asset classes).

## 3. How it is done (the published process chain — verbatim steps, plus domain context)

Published chain (SIR 2026 Protocol page, steps 1–6, quoted in substance — this is the company's own published sequence, and the datasets do not add steps to it):

1. **The asset:** weight, dimensions, centre of mass, and value at risk are established first.
2. **The structure:** route and clearance survey at both ends, stairs and landings measured, and floor deflection assessed against L/360 — span divided by 360 — before any lift is planned.
3. **The load path:** load spreading and protection runs are planned where the substrate needs support, and floors, walls, and frames are protected end to end.
4. **The crew and rigging are matched to the plan** — dollies, straps, and equipment chosen for this asset and this route.
5. **The move:** controlled, strapped, and supervised along the planned path.
6. **Placement:** the asset is set in its final position, levelled, and verified with the client — at the flat rate agreed in writing.

The structural protocol page compresses the same chain into three phases (Assess the asset → Assess the structure → Move under KIM-GS) and adds two published details: calibration-sensitive and fragile high-mass items are flagged at the assess stage, "not discovered on the day"; and where a topic is export or freight, packing and crating are to ISPM 15 (cross-ref to the ISPM 15 category, not a SIR claim).

Domain context around the chain (researched 2026-10-29, labeled domain — none of it is company equipment or practice):
- **Load spreading:** heavy wood skids or plywood decking spread a concentrated load across more joists/floor area (domain practice for heavy deliveries). The company's published equivalent is "load spreading and protection runs are planned where the substrate needs support" — the domain examples illustrate the idea; they are not the company's equipment list.
- **Rigging equipment inventory (domain only):** safe-moving dollies in the 3,000–4,000 lb class (pairs to 6,000 lb), powered stair climbers (to the 3,500 lb class in listings), straps and tie-downs, gantries and hoists for basement work, cribbing. The company publishes ONLY "dollies, straps and equipment" + warehouse forklift/track loader (industrial-page photo caption). Stair climbers, gantries, cranes, and air bearings are NOT claimed as company equipment (§8 Q4).
- **Stair practice (domain):** inspect the stairs themselves before the move (a heavy asset plus crew weight can exceed what wooden stairs tolerate); keep per-person loads low; restrain the load on stairs (e.g., a strap anchored at the top) so a slip does not become a runaway. Domain guidance, not company procedure.
- **Protection runs (published + domain):** published: floors, walls, and frames protected end to end. Domain materials: floor runners, doorway protection, rail protection, blanket wrap, stretch wrap — the same protection vocabulary the residential datasets carry.
- **Verification (published):** placement is levelled and verified WITH the client. That client-verification step is the published close-out; no separate inspection, sign-off document, or warranty is published, and none is claimed (§8).
- **What the chain does NOT include (published silence, recorded, not filled):** no step publishes a PSI calculation, a joist schedule, an engineering stamp, a re-anchoring step, a calibration step, or a post-move inspection report. Re-anchoring, calibration, and engineer involvement are all gated (§8 Q5 and the gun-safe package's Q5/Q7, cross-ref only).
